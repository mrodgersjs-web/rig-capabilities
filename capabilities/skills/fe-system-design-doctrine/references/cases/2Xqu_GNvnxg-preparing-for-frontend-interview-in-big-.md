## Author's framework as used
1. Fix the goal first: know what you want to achieve, how, and build the list of companies you will apply to, the list tells you what their interviews expect. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@00:59)
2. Set a deadline (start and end) and a daily time budget, so study topics can be spread across the window instead of crammed. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@01:21)
3. Share what you learn publicly to hold motivation; discipline, not motivation, is what carries the effort through to the end. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@01:44) CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@01:58)
4. Work the four big-tech rounds in order: algorithmic coding, front-end coding, system design, behavioral. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@03:00)
5. Close with a timeline summary per round and the total prep estimate. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@17:28)

## Worked design
There is no design case in this video; the worked object is the preparation plan itself. The dimensions below map onto what he actually covers.

Requirements (goal and scope)
- Clear personal goal plus a company list, since preparation length depends on how many companies you target. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@00:59) CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@18:17)
- A deadline and daily time commitment to enforce consistency. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@01:21)

Components (the four rounds)
- Algorithmic coding round: standard computer-science problem solving with specific algorithms and data structures. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@03:30)
- Front-end coding round: JavaScript, HTML, CSS fundamentals and browser mechanics, framework-agnostic. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@06:28)
- System design round: he calls it one of his favorite parts and the reason the channel exists; each company runs its own version. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@09:31)
- Behavioral round: soft skills, experience, how you work with people. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@13:47)

Data model (how he organizes practice)
- Problems grouped by pattern and data structure rather than solved at random. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@05:40)
- A structured LeetCode-style list he credits with keeping his own prep organized. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@05:56)
- A personal Notion document with resource lists, per-topic notes, widget examples, and a behavioral question list. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@07:04) CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@08:55) CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@15:58)

API / realtime, not covered.

Performance (prep efficiency)
- Widget building and app cloning compress system-design learning because you can explain what you have implemented yourself. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@10:55)
- One month for front-end problems versus two to three months for algorithms, the round closest to daily work is the fastest to prepare. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@08:18)

Accessibility, not covered. His one inclusive-audience note is the non-native-English vocabulary warning. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@16:15)

## Trade-offs he names
- Narrow company list vs broad applications: a limited list (two or three companies) shortens the timeline; more companies stretch it. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@18:17)
- Front-end system design vs API/backend system design: focus on front-end first, since backend design is an entirely different subject with plenty of its own material. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@13:15)
- Honesty vs a polished story: admitting you lack a good story and offering a related one beats fabricating, both for truthfulness and because it moves the interview forward. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@15:07)
- Motivation vs discipline: early motivation is common, but the discipline built in the first two months is what decides the outcome. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@01:44)

## Anti-patterns he warns about
- Lying in a behavioral interview: an interviewer can probe any story deeply, and being caught is a red flag that can undo the rest of your loop. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@14:52)
- Treating "conflict" as a scary word and misreading the question, it describes professional disagreement, for example colleagues preferring their own architecture. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@16:39)
- Reaching for framework knowledge (React, Angular) to pass a fundamentals round where it will not help. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@06:40)
- Studying without a target-company list, which makes it impossible to know what to expect. CLAIM(2Xqu_GNvnxg@01:07)
