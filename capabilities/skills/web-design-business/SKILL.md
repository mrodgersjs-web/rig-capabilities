# Web Design Business

## Method

Pick a business model before you pick a price. Self-Made Web Designer maps the field clearly: there is a low-price, high-volume model where designers charge little per site and churn out many; a high-price, low-volume model doing bespoke work with extra skills attached; a productized model with a fixed outcome, fixed timeline, and fixed price; and a subscription model where clients pay a monthly fee for a set amount of work. Each model prices, sells, and delivers differently, and mixing them is how studios end up doing bespoke work at volume prices.

Positioning is who and what, in one sentence. Not "we build beautiful websites" (true of every competitor) but "we build booking sites for independent dental practices". The test: if a competitor could say your line verbatim without lying, the line is not doing its job. Paige Brunton's guest tightened her niche to service-based providers and kept a deliberate escape hatch: she still takes interesting work outside the niche. A niche is a focus for selling, not a cage for delivery.

Where the money is: buyers who value outcomes over decoration, found where they discuss their own problems. Her interview about premium website clients is titled bluntly about where those clients actually are and opens by saying they are not on Instagram liking your posts. Premium work comes from being findable by a narrow buyer (niche content, referrals, communities they run in) and from proof you can show (named case studies with outcomes).

The money mechanics are non-negotiable and they go in before work starts:

1. A written contract. Even a template-level contract beats a handshake. Self-Made Web Designer's lawyer episode exists because designers skip contracts out of fear and then land in hot water over the terms nobody wrote down.
2. A deposit before any work begins. Will Paterson's studio practice is a deposit required before starting work (UNVERIFIED numeric: his caption says 50%, and this claim comes from videoId j0O676O_-gs at [07:28], a title I am not reproducing because it contains an emoji character).
3. Milestone payments tied to sign-offs, not to your mood.
4. A revision cap written into the deal (see web-design-business-client).
5. A written fee for out-of-scope work, so scope growth becomes a change order instead of an argument.

Pipeline discipline: keep a named prospect list, not a vague intention to "post more". Ask for the budget on every discovery call so you learn what this buyer actually pays before you spend a week on a proposal. Track win rate and lost-reason per deal; fix the offer and the positioning first, because effort is the last variable to blame.

## Anti-patterns

- Everything-store: selling logos, sites, SEO, and social management, which signals to a premium buyer that nothing is your craft.
- Model mixing: charging productized prices for bespoke work or bespoke hours for productized promises.
- Handshake economics: starting work on a verbal yes and discovering the payment terms after delivery.
- Audience cosplay: hanging out in designer communities and mistaking that for pipeline.
- Effort excuse: blaming "not posting enough" when the offer names no buyer and no outcome.
