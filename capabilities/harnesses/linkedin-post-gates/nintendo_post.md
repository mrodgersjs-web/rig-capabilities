I blew into a Nintendo cartridge for ten years and I have no idea if it ever worked.

That was the ritual. Blow in it, slot it, press down, pray. Red blinking light. Blow again. Slot it again. Then the theme music hits and you forget you were ever worried.

Here is the part I never thought about until this month.

The console was plug and play. One box, one job, no configuration. But the console alone was nothing. You could own a Nintendo for a year and play exactly one game. The console was the platform.

The cartridges were the experiments.

Every game was $40 and a weekend. Contra was a bet. Excitebike was a bet. You didn't know if you'd love it until you were three levels in. Some were duds. You ate the $40 and you learned something about what you liked. That was the whole model.
And then there was World 1-1.

Shigeru Miyamoto built the first level of Super Mario Bros. as a classroom. The first Goomba walks at you slowly, in an empty stretch of level, with nothing else on screen. You can die there. It costs you nothing. He put the danger in a safe place on purpose so you would learn the whole control scheme by playing instead of by reading a manual.

That is a pilot. That is what testing the water looks like when someone smart designs it.
Now here is what I see in every company that asks me about AI.
They want to buy the console. They want the platform decision, the enterprise license, the committee, the rollout, one vendor, one big bet, one number in the budget. They want the thing to be plug and play.
Then they never insert a cartridge.

The console is sitting under the TV. It's on. It's beautiful. The red light is green. And nobody has played a single game, so nobody knows whether Mario jumps right, and nobody ever will, because the only way to find out is to slot one in and press start.

Forty dollars. One weekend. That was always the price of finding out.
Your version of that is cheaper now. Pick one annoying repetitive thing your team does every day and hates. Run it through a model. Not a platform. Not a rollout. One cartridge. If it saves twenty minutes, you learned something. If it hallucinates, you learned something. Either way you're out fourteen cents and you know more than you did yesterday.

Then slot in the next one.

The console was never the point. The cartridges were. Nobody ever bought a Nintendo to look at the console.
