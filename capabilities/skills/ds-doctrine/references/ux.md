## Disagreements between creators (who says what, cited)

- How far to push originality: DesignCourse tells designers not to be original with nav and logo placement (CLAIM(D29KL52Srtw@05:40)), while Satori Graphics pushes breaking the sector's color convention to stand out (CLAIM(-5jSCHZIHlg@04:20)). Reading: structure stays conventional, brand color may break convention; the two rules operate on different layers.
- Hover intensity: DesignCourse's nav lesson allows bold, underline, icon, or border changes on hover (CLAIM(D29KL52Srtw@03:07)), while its forms lesson insists button hover stay a subtle lightening (CLAIM(5-caRWzSQd4@23:13)). Reading: link hover may add emphasis; a filled button should barely move.
- Testing protocol: Mike Locke has the tester narrate their intended taps rather than perform them (CLAIM(9viTvO0Okoo@03:05)), while DesignSense has users try the shipped app and report what happened (CLAIM(P2W2AnK5aDE@14:10)). Reading: narrated intent is fast and cheap for a personal project; observed use plus feedback is the stronger evidence when the product is real.
- Delight versus safety: Self-Made Web Designer warns that an all-safe design bores the visitor and prescribes small surprises (CLAIM(d-IaU9qcDGg@04:29)), while Satori Graphics warns that any strong contrast point drains attention from the rest of the design (CLAIM(-5jSCHZIHlg@05:02)). Reading: micro-interactions and single-point emphasis are compatible if only one element is isolated and the delight stays small.
- Icon versus words: Jesse Showalter says replace a paragraph with an icon when the icon already carries the meaning (CLAIM(w6qyekgIo8I@03:13)), while Flux Academy says never convey an instruction or state through an icon alone, and replace an unclear icon with a plain word (CLAIM(EDtgsIn1meM@08:26), CLAIM(TKZl_UlXhYk@04:43)). Reading: an icon may replace a paragraph only when the meaning is already conventional; if the meaning is unclear, use the word.
- Working-memory cap: Self-Made Web Designer cites about three or four items (CLAIM(d-IaU9qcDGg@06:13)); Jesse Showalter cites seven plus or minus two, and also says not to pad up to that count (CLAIM(eQGjV6_8t7I@04:33), CLAIM(eQGjV6_8t7I@05:15)). Reading: both agree fewer is safer; use the smaller chunk (three or four) for a buy decision, and never fill a list to hit seven.
- How hard cancel may be: Jesse Showalter puts tertiary actions out of the main path but still reachable (CLAIM(w6qyekgIo8I@01:09)). Orizon Design mutes the cancel control the same way, then warns that hiding it, or stacking more screens after a refused save, crosses into a dark pattern (CLAIM(yWkuZj0OO-o@02:33), CLAIM(yWkuZj0OO-o@04:27)). Reading: quieter than keep, never hidden, and stop the save sequence once the person has refused.
- Progress indication: Jesse Showalter says split a complex job into steps so people are not overloaded (CLAIM(eQGjV6_8t7I@02:09)). Orizon Design says a progress bar that barely moves can raise drop-off by advertising how much work remains (CLAIM(9Y_qqmu2QiM@03:11)). Reading: use steps, and if you show a bar, each step must move it.
- Confidence versus proof: Sam Crawford says do not be humble on your own site, and that saying you are the best is fine even if you are not sure it is true (CLAIM(kvcEhzoipZs@07:18), CLAIM(kvcEhzoipZs@08:06)). The same video shows his page still citing an older site count (CLAIM(kvcEhzoipZs@11:06)). Reading: claim the outcome and do not list competitors, but do not ship a superlative or a count you cannot defend today.
